## 3 · What changed since round 10

### 3.1 · The two families of engagement

**What you asked.** "the engagements should be divided into two types": "The comprehensive engagements, which are
obviously the three engagement tiers we have", and "Micro engagements, or we could give it a more specific name". The
smaller ones are "small-scale individual projects, based on the client's requirements and needs at any given moment",
priced lower, as a way in and "an upsell opportunity". You named email deliverability, cold emailing, cold calling,
conference activation and executive dinners.

**The structure**

| Page | Address | What it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Engagements | /engagements/ | What does working with Kelwin involve, and what can I buy? Both families side by side, and Launch, Growth and Scaling in full. |
| Launch, Growth, Scaling | /engagements/#launch, #growth, #scaling | Sections of Engagements, each with its own link to send after a call. |
| How an engagement runs | /engagements/#how-it-runs | The method's four steps on the six-month timeline, written out as well as drawn. A section, not a separate page: a link to send, and text an assistant can quote. |
| The fixed-scope engagements (the index, formerly Projects) | /engagements/fixed-scope/ | Is there a smaller first step? Every one in one list, how one runs, and where it leads. |
| One page per fixed-scope engagement | /engagements/ plus the buyer's words, for example /engagements/email-deliverability/ | What exactly does this one thing involve, who is it for, and how do I ask for it? |

One page for both families would put two different buyers on one long page: the CEO weighing a six-month retainer,
and the head of sales who needs one thing fixed this month. Two unlinked pages would read as two companies. **So: one
Engagements menu with both families in it**, and the way up from a fixed-scope engagement to a package always in view.

**No package pages at launch.** Buyers search for a problem or a service, not for "Launch package" (our judgment).
Each package's section has its own link. A package page comes later, only if the offer makes the packages differ in
kind and buyers ask for a page about one of them.

**When a fixed-scope engagement gets its own page.** All four must hold:

1. Buyers search for it by name. This is our judgment for now; we check search volumes before the new pages are built.
2. We deliver it today, with the people you have lined up.
3. It has enough of its own to say: its scope, who it fits and who it doesn't, its steps, what the client keeps and a
   specimen. Take away the sections every such page shares, and more than one screen is still left.
4. It ends at a door.

One stated exception: a page that is sent after a call rather than found by search. The operating model is that page.
It is sent after an audit's readout.

Why the tests: pages made from one template with a word swapped are what Google's rules call doorways, and treat as
spam [2]. A candidate that fails becomes a row on the index that opens in place, with its text in the page.

**The candidates.** The offer decides which launch, their names and their scope.

**Account-based marketing.** You asked whether Scaling's one-to-one play for up to 100 named accounts is
account-based marketing. Yes, that is account-based marketing. The offer rewrites it (about ten accounts one-to-one,
the rest in clusters) and says where each kind sits. The later page is called "Account-based marketing", at
/engagements/account-based-marketing/.

**Add-ons inside the packages** are rows in each package's section, named, with no prices and no pages: a calling
channel, a webinar, a team training session, executive dinners on Growth and Scaling, an extra show. Conference
activation is both: included in every package, and sold on its own.

**How a fixed-scope engagement starts, and its doors.** The offer's lifecycle starts every fixed-scope engagement with
a free scope check: up to three working days on the one piece, then a 30-minute readout. On each page:

- **The quiet door, "Ask about this engagement",** near the top and in the close. It opens the contact form with the
  engagement chosen. A buyer who arrives from a search for one service is often ready to act, and a fast answer
  matters most then: in one study of 2,241 US firms, those that answered an online inquiry within an hour were about
  seven times as likely to qualify the lead as those that answered later [3]. So we propose a reply **within one
  working day for any message that names an engagement**, the audit's standard, instead of the two working days for
  general questions. Once the calendar is live, soon after launch, the acknowledgement email also carries a link to
  book the 30-minute first call. This needs the lifecycle's yes.
- **"How it runs"** on the page describes the scope check, so the page needs no form of its own.
- **The red audit door in the close says what it is for here,** in the voice work's words, along the lines of "More
  than one part breaking? That is what the System Audit is for." A buyer with one problem isn't sent to a ten-day
  diagnosis.

**Names and addresses.** Every address is chosen once, before launch. Google follows a forward from an old address,
but a forward made by the page itself is a weaker signal than one made by the server, and any forward takes time to be
picked up [4]. So we choose once. The family's name is in no
address. /engagements/fixed-scope/ says how everything in it is bought, a fixed scope at a fixed fee, and each
engagement's own address uses the words a buyer types. Projects was never on the public site, so moving it costs
nothing.

### One sales process, being redone (today's two paths, until then)

You asked for one thorough, premium sales process for both families: "I feel like the sales process should be very
similar for both kinds of engagements". The offer's lifecycle does that: the same nine steps, sized in time. The doors
on the pages stay the same whichever way it lands.

- **Launch, Growth and Scaling:** the audit's questions → a 30-minute first call → ten working days → the readout, on
  a 60-minute call → the proposal → the pilot (months 1–3) → the full run (months 4–6) → renewal.
- **A fixed-scope engagement:** "Ask about this engagement" → a reply within one working day (proposed) → the
  30-minute first call → the free scope check, up to three working days → a 30-minute readout → the written scope →
  the stop after week one → the build, with a check 30 days on → upkeep, or the audit and then a package.

### The inbound machine (new)

You asked for "automations and link things together to make the website a fully fledged lead gen machine for inbound
efforts". From launch, these run on their own:

1. **Every form lands in the CRM within a minute,** as one record per person, with the page, the form and where the
   visit came from, including "An AI assistant" when they say so.
2. **Every form sends an instant acknowledgement** from the right mailbox, saying what happens next and by when.
3. **Leads that fit raise an alert.** An audit request that scores high goes to your phone. Every other message goes to
   whoever owns the inbox that day, and any reply past its time is flagged each morning.
4. **Reminders before every booked first call,** once the calendar is live.
5. **The monthly notes email is drafted from the feed,** the Lab waitlist is reviewed each month for companies that
   fit, and a one-page summary of the numbers arrives every Monday.

**What stays with a person, and why.** Every reply to a buyer who fits, every follow-up after the first
acknowledgement, and every send to a list. A firm that sells high-touch work is judged by who writes back. The speed
comes from the alerts, not from automated emails, and your rule stands: no automated email pretends to be a person. We
don't propose loosening any of the follow-up rules below. The machine makes the people faster, not fewer. The tools,
the scoring and the alerts are in the behind-the-site document.
