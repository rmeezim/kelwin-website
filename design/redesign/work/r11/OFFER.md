## 1 · The short answer

**Keep the three packages and what's in them. Keep Launch at $8,000. Raise Growth to $20,000. Don't quote Scaling until
it is costed.** Kelwin isn't giving too little. Belkins sells meetings, priced by appointments a year, and most of the
extras you saw are paid add-ons or partner work. Kelwin sells the system the meetings come from, in the client's own
accounts. So we add depth that costs us little, and price the rest:

- **Newly included:** conference activation in every package; training the client's team on what we wrote; the
  named-account work called what it is (account-based).
- **Priced add-ons,** one price each, at most three on a proposal: executive dinners (Growth and Scaling), webinars,
  retargeting, more named-account depth, team training.
- **Never cold texts or WhatsApp.** Both need the person's consent first. Reminders only, to people who said yes.
- **Scaling's one-to-one play for 100 accounts promised more than its price covers.** It becomes about 10 accounts
  one-to-one and the rest in clusters.

**The honest economics.** Costed seat by seat at middle contractor rates, the first six months of a package roughly
break even once the free audit is counted. The profit is in the full-run months, renewals and the fixed-scope
engagements. Growth at $15,000 loses money; at $20,000 it earns about what Launch does. Scaling's two markets look like
350–400 contractor hours a month, far more than "from $25,000" pays for.

**The smaller family becomes "fixed-scope engagements"** ("micro" makes the work sound small), **and the packages
"retained engagements".** Nine at launch: your six, the outbound build (our answer to cold email), conference
activation, and executive dinners on request. No cold calling or monthly cold email on their own.

**Your pilot by commitment is sound, as three ways to start, chosen on one call:** six months fixed (55%), six months
open at day 90 (65%, recommended), or month to month (the full fee plus 10%). One meeting fee, $250, with one cap.
"Leave at any time" at 70% would cost less than month to month every month, so it becomes month to month.

**The day-90 exit, by option.** On the open option, a client may leave at day 90 for any reason. If we met every
written target and it leaves anyway, it pays the $3,000 readiness fee, so the build is paid for once. On the fixed
option, it leaves free only if we missed a target; otherwise it pays back the pilot discount, as round 10 had it.

**Revenue share: 3% on deals closed within 12 months of the first meeting, as you proposed, capped at 75% of the fees
billed in the first six months.** The cap can never be more than three quarters of what the client paid us, so a client
who leaves early gets a smaller one. Round 10's fixed ceilings go.

**No setup fee on six-month terms. The deposit drops to one pilot month** ($8,800 at signing on Launch, fixed, against
$13,200). **Every option shows everything a buyer could ever pay us on one line.**

**Who leads.** You sell. A contractor, the GTM and revenue-architecture specialist, leads each engagement, with a named
backup. How much we take on is set in hours, from each contractor's confirmed hours, which we still need from you.

### 2.2 How the two connect

- **One sale for both, the same steps, scaled in time.** That answers your note that "the sales process should be very
  similar for both kinds of engagements". LIFECYCLE.md sets it out. Two of its rules shape this offer:
  - Every fixed-scope engagement begins with a free scope check, about 3 hours of contractor time. Under about
    $10,000, the written scope and the order form go out with the scope readout, the proposal call is optional, and
    the aim is a signature within five working days of the first call.
  - We recommend the audit only when more than one layer is breaking (R10). A fixed-scope engagement must never feel
    like bait for a bigger one.
- **What selling costs us before a signature** (middle rates; your own selling time isn't counted). An audit and its
  proposal take about 13 contractor hours, about $1,700. If one audit in two becomes a client, that is about $3,400 per
  signed package; at one in three, about $5,100. A scope check costs about $375, so about $750 per signed fixed-scope
  client at one in two. The size question on the audit and the fit test on the first call are how we aim for one in
  two (our arithmetic).
- **The credit toward a package** (LIFECYCLE 4.4). Half the fee, up to one month of the package's full fee, in equal
  parts on months 4, 5 and 6, if the package is signed within 90 days of handover. Only our fee counts; outside costs
  and upkeep don't. Only work the package reuses earns it: deliverability, positioning, the ICP and signal map, the CRM
  blueprint, win/loss, the operating model and the outbound build. Events don't. On Growth, at most $5,000 until the
  first Growth client's hours are known. It lapses by itself at a day-90 exit.
- **Who the fixed-scope builds are for.** A buyer who wants positioning, the ICP map and outbound all together pays
  less through the Launch pilot ($22,600 even if it leaves at day 90 without cause) than buying the three separately
  ($39,000–47,000). That is intended: the pilot is the better way to buy the whole system, and the readiness fee means
  the build is paid for once. The fixed-scope builds are for teams that will run the work themselves, or that need one
  piece. Upkeep ($3,500 a month) sits below the cheapest pilot month ($4,400).

- **Within the first six months, a quarterly cap pays the same or less than a pooled one,** and pays less whenever
  deals bunch into one quarter, which big deals do. What gives you more upside than round 10 is 75% instead of 50%, not
  the quarter. So if the aim is "to keep our upside motivating enough", choose the six-month version you listed first.
  More upside could come later, by carrying the share into a 12- or 24-month renewal with its own cap (6.3).
- **The pilot quarter has the smallest bucket, even though sourced deals begin there too.** Pooling the six months
  avoids that.
- **The caps rarely bind.** In round 10's typical example the Launch share is about $6,450. The cap matters for big
  single wins, like yours, and for the buyer's peace of mind.

### 6.3 Defined precisely

- **Sourced:** the date of the held first meeting on the signed monthly statement: a meeting that took place, inside
  the written customer profile, where our outreach made the first contact, not on the do-not-count list, and not
  rejected within five working days (R10, approved). The company then goes on the sourcing list. A later meeting with
  the same company never restarts its clock.
- **Which company:** the legal entity, or the business unit, whose people attended, not its whole group. (The
  do-not-count list still counts a company with its affiliates, which protects the client.)
- **Events:** a meeting our outreach books at a show counts like any other held meeting. A seat at a dinner never
  counts; a separate first meeting booked after the dinner, with a guest whose company our outreach first contacted,
  does. Webinar sign-ups are "influenced", never billed. A fixed-scope event carries no meeting fee and no share.
- **Closed:** the earliest of the signature on the first contract, a signed letter of intent, or the start of service.
  The client agrees not to delay a signature to avoid the share.
- **The window:** the deal must close within 12 months of the held meeting. Your example works exactly: a meeting in
  June 2026 and a signature in May 2027 is in; a signature after June 2027 is out. Each statement line prints its own
  deadline. A company with an open opportunity at proposal stage or later on its deadline still counts if it closes
  within 90 more days.
- **After an exit for our miss** (a missed day-90 target, or the months 4–6 meetings target missed twice): only deals
  that close within six months of the meeting count.
- **What is counted:** 3% of the first-year value of the first contract, and of anything else that company signs
  within six months of it. A paid or free pilot that leads to the main contract within six months counts as part of
  it. Later expansion and renewals don't count. First-year value is the greater of what the client invoices that
  customer in its first 12 months, and the contract's total value divided by its years. For usage-priced revenue, the
  first 12 months invoiced. Purchases through a reseller, distributor or marketplace count, and so do contracts signed
  by the client's affiliates.
- **The cap:** 75% of the fees billed for months 1–6: the monthly fee, monthly add-ons and meeting fees, but not
  per-event fees, the readiness fee or outside costs. On Launch's open option that is $36,000 at most, $32,700 in the
  typical case, and $14,700 for a client that leaves at day 90. The share can never be more than three quarters of what
  the client paid us in its first six months. The same rule on every option, month to month included (on meetings in
  its first six months).
- **How it is checked:** the client signs each monthly meeting statement. Both sides sign the full sourcing list at
  month 6, and at any exit. Each quarter after that, until the last window closes, the client's finance lead signs a
  short statement listing every company on the sourcing list as "no deal", "open" or "signed", with the signature date
  and first-year value. No order forms change hands. A false statement is a breach of contract. Kelwin may ask about
  any listed company and gets an answer within 10 working days. Once, after the last window closes, an independent
  accountant may check the records of listed companies only; Kelwin pays unless underpayment is more than 5% or $2,000,
  in which case the client pays. Late statements and late payments carry interest. No CRM access is needed after the
  contract ends.
- **How it is invoiced:** half when that customer pays its first invoice, half six months later if it is still a
  customer, due in 30 days, separately from the monthly fee (R10).
- **After the engagement ends:** the clause survives every way of ending, and binds anyone who buys the client's
  business. The window is fixed per meeting, so a renewal doesn't stretch it. The share covers meetings held in the
  first six months, whatever the option. Renewals are monthly fee only unless the renewal says otherwise (R10,
  approved). If you want more upside later, the same rule can carry into a 12- or 24-month renewal, with its own cap.
- **Paying twice.** A buyer will say they already pay their rep a commission on the same deal. One sentence in the
  proposal: "Your reps' commission stays as it is. The 3% is our part for creating the first meeting, a small slice of
  the deal's first year, and never more than three quarters of what you paid us in the first six months."

### 6.4 Better terms for Growth and Scaling?

Recommended: **one rule for all three tiers, with one exception on Scaling.**

- **The cap already grows with the package.** At 75% of fees billed, Growth's reaches $90,000 at $20,000, against round
  10's fixed $50,000. Drop the fixed ceilings. That alone is the better term you asked for.
- **Don't raise the rate.** Larger clients have procurement teams and lawyers who fight variable fees hardest, and 3% on
  a larger client's larger deals already earns more in dollars. A buyer will say: "I'm paying you more, and you want a
  bigger cut?"
- **The exception: an 18-month window for Scaling's one-to-one accounts only** (about 10). These are the slowest,
  largest deals, worked account by account, and a 12-month window would cut off the deals the program exists for. The
  accounts are named in a schedule signed at kickoff; only companies on that schedule get 18 months. A buyer will say:
  "Fair. Those deals are slow, and you're working them by hand."
- What a buyer hears on every tier: "The share can never be more than three quarters of what you paid us in the first
  six months." That is the fairness test buyers apply (R10: Keränen and others, 2023).

### 6.5 Will it help close deals faster?

You asked: "How likely would it be to help us close the deals faster?" Honestly, unlikely. In a 2026 survey, 76% of
large buyers said they were open to paying for outcomes, but only 5% had such a contract (R10: hfsresearch.com). A share
adds a tail, a check and a clause that outlives the contract, which lawyers read longest (T, our reading). What closes
faster is a low-risk first step: the exit and a known maximum (T, R10). The share's job is to give Kelwin a fair part of
a big win and to show confidence. Keep it to one sentence in the proposal and one paragraph in the contract, and never
lead with it. What we'd tell a buyer: "We earn 3% of the first year of any deal signed within 12 months of a first
meeting we created, and never more than three quarters of what you paid us in the first six months."
