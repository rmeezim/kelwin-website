## 1 · The short answer

1. **We keep every craft rule you liked:** a doer in every claim, plain words, real numbers, no metaphors, no long
   dashes, sourced or labeled proof, the honesty test and the checklist.
2. **We change what the doer acts on.** Round 10 had "we" acting on emails, decks and domains, which is how an
   agency describes itself. Now "we" acts on your buyers' decisions and on Kelwin's named assets: Narrative OS, the
   Signal Dashboard, the Rebuild Sequence.
3. **First screens show what we see and the asset that holds it.** Meeting rhythms, packages, contract terms and
   who sends the emails move to the Engagements page and the proposal.
4. **Reassurance and the agency comparison come out of the pitch.** No sign-off loops, no "in your name" or "for
   you", no line that starts with "free", and no agencies as the foil.
5. **The test reader is a CEO reading on a phone between calls, not a ten-year-old.** We use the buyer's own
   professional words and never shrink our work ("short", "a folder", "just that one thing").
6. **Close service and our own method show through structure, never adjectives:** who is in the room, the
   standards we keep, the readout presented live, and what we refuse.
7. **No slogans of our own.** Three lines the first draft kept repeating read as an agency or a platform: "we run it
   with your team", "a limited number of engagements" and "the system their revenue runs on". They are retired (T15,
   T16).

**Why it read as an agency.** Fixing the AI feel gave every sentence a real doer and a real object. In a GTM firm,
the most concrete objects are emails, inboxes, decks and domains, so the pages turned into a list of tasks. The
counts show it:

| Per 1,000 words | Narrative Systems, round 10 | Homepage you approved |
|---|---|---|
| "We" + a task verb (write, send, set up, rewrite, check...) | 19 | 3 |
| Channel words (email, inbox, domain, deck, slide, reply...) | 18 | 6 |
| Words for the system and its named assets | 2 | 28 to 35 |

The homepage already speaks as a partner with its own method: "Revenue is an architectural problem.", named assets
with a type each, a diagnosis before any offer, and a gate that turns people away. The middle ground you asked for
is close to that register, written with the round-10 craft.

## 2 · Who is speaking

### 2.1 The stance

**We are the founding team of Kelwin, a GTM systems partner.** We build and run the system behind what a B2B
company's buyers hear, whom it approaches and why its deals close. The system lives in the company's own accounts and
documents. We are not selling hours of work (an agency), a finished deliverable (a vendor) or a framework (a
consultancy).

| Trait | How it shows on the page |
|---|---|
| A partner who builds and runs the client's revenue system | Retainer first. We say we run it, and where it lives, as plain facts. |
| With its own method and instruments | We name the six assets and the four phases of the method, and we show each asset as a specimen. |
| Selective | The audit is a gate. We say whom we take on and whom we turn away, and why. |
| Calm | No urgency, no superlatives, no chasing. One door to the audit per screen. |
| Exact | Decided numbers, sourced figures, the reader's own professional words. |
| Looks after the client | We show care through who is in the room, the standards we keep and the record we write, never with adjectives. |

- **"We" is Kelwin.** Never "I". Never a name. Never "the founder" meaning us.
- **People are "the founding team"** when a sentence needs people. No headcount, no invented people or titles.
- **The reader is "you".** In specimens and client documents, the reader is "the client" or a role ("your head of
  sales").

**The reader.** The CEO, founder, head of sales or CFO of a B2B company that has revenue and sells to buying
committees. Reads on a phone between calls. Uses "pipeline", "champion" and "win rate" every day. Has spent a
quarter on activity and ended it where they started. Doesn't know our names yet.

**The reader's questions, and where each is answered**
- On first screens and capability pages: What do you see in companies like mine? What is the system, and what
  does it decide? Why should I believe you? How do we start?
- On Engagements, at the audit's door and in the proposal: What do you need from me? Who does the work day to day?
  What does it commit me to? What if it doesn't work? How is it bought?

Round 10 answered the second group on every first screen. That is the order of a statement of work, and it is the
main reason the pages read as an agency.

## 3 · The agency tells, and what we write instead

Fourteen patterns were tested on round 10's pages, and thirteen were found. Two more (T15 and T16) turned up in
this round's own first drafts. Each entry gives the pattern, one or two of our own lines before, the line after, and
why. The "After" lines are illustrations in this voice. Lines from pages you approved
(the homepage, the audit FAQ) still need your yes, and the seven homepage lines you said yes to rewriting are
written in their own document this round.

### T1 · Selling labor: "we + a task + your channel thing"

- **Before** (Narrative Systems, the sentences under the headline): "With you, we write down what you sell, who it's
  for and why buyers should pick you. We test the wording in your first emails, then rewrite your website, deck and
  call guides around the winner. After that, we keep testing new wording every month."
- **After:** "Narrative OS is your positioning and messaging as one written record, with the proof behind each claim.
  We build it from what buyers said when they chose you or turned you down. A line stays only while that evidence
  supports it."
- **Why.** A list of tasks is priced per task and compared with longer lists for less money. It also tells the
  reader a copywriter could do it. The new lines name the asset, what it decides and where its evidence comes from.

### T2 · The channel leads, and the system follows

- **Before** (Narrative Systems, first screen): "Who sends the emails · We do, from inboxes in your company's name;
  your reps take the replies"
- **After:** the line leaves the first screen. Where the engagement is described, once the business model settles
  who sends: "Outreach goes only to accounts on the ICP & Signal Map, and your CRM records every reply."
- **Why.** On the page for the most strategic of the three capabilities, the first fact was the promise of an
  outbound appointment-setting firm. Kelwin does run outreach, so we say it, in its place, by the rule that governs
  it. The first draft of this guide wrote "Outreach is one output of the system", then stacked three capitalized
  asset names. That is how list-building and GTM-engineering shops pitch themselves, and the stack of names reads as
  platform talk (T16).

### T3 · Meeting rhythms as the pitch

- **Before:** "From you · Your CEO and head of sales, in our working session (every two weeks on Launch, weekly on
  Growth)" and "Each week we spend 25 minutes with whoever owns pipeline on your side, going through the dashboard."
- **After** (once, on Engagements): "Every engagement has a weekly review and a quarterly review with your
  leadership." On GTM Intelligence: "Each week we review the Signal Dashboard and write down what moved." Meeting
  lengths, who attends and per-package rhythms go in the proposal.
- **Why.** Meetings and their lengths read as account management. The buyer pictures a calendar, not senior people
  working on their revenue, and starts asking for a cheaper tier with fewer meetings.

### T4 · The service menu: what you get, how it works, which tier

- **Before:** the rows "What we do · ... What you do · ... What you get · ..." under "Months 1 to 3", "Months 4 to 6"
  and "From month 7, if you renew"; and "Can we start with just the documents? Yes. We can write the positioning,
  the message matrix and the proof list as a one-off project."
- **After:** the stages become the asset's history, with each edition as the subject. "The first edition starts
  from recent buyers you introduce us to, those who chose you and those who didn't, then from your leadership." The
  smaller option moves to the Engagements page: "Positioning and messaging is also an engagement of its own, with a
  written scope and the same method."
- **Before** (round-10 guide, Projects): "If you need one thing fixed, we can do just that one thing."
- **After:** "Some companies need one part of the system first. We take that part on by itself, with the same
  method."
- **Why.** A menu invites the buyer to pick the smallest item and ask what each one costs. "One-off" and "just
  that one thing" sound like a freelancer. A process told as our verbs in order (interview, write, test, roll out)
  is the same menu with new nouns, so each stage's subject is the edition. "To the same standard" is the smaller
  plan's reassurance (the same quality in a smaller package), so we name the method instead.

### T5 · Reassurance: sign-off, agreement, safety

- **Before:** "Your marketing lead signs off each page, slide and email before it goes live, and your reps join the
  training." and "together we agree a ranked list of changes."
- **After:** "Each quarter we bring your leadership the Rebuild Sequence, the ranked plan of what to change next,
  with the evidence for each item. Your leadership decides which items go ahead." Sign-off goes in the contract,
  and in one direct answer on Engagements: "Who approves what goes public? Your team does, in writing, before
  anything goes live."
- **Why.** Each reassurance answers a fear buyers have about agencies (lock-in, wasted tools, a supplier who
  publishes without asking). Answering those fears up front confirms the category. Approval loops also make the
  client our editor, which is a supplier relationship.

### T6 · Outsourcing words: "in your name", "for you"

- **Before:** "Everything we set up for you sits in your accounts and in your name." (the sixth line of the round-10
  short version) and "the first emails we send for you".
- **After:** "The system lives in your own accounts and documents." Said once per page at most. "We run it" needs no
  partner clause (T15).
- **Why.** "In your name" is how outsourced sales teams and ghostwriters describe themselves. A senior buyer hears
  someone else emailing as their company. The fact underneath is strong, and it stays: what we build lives in the
  client's own accounts.

### T8 · Agencies as the foil

- **Before** (round-10 About rewrite): "Kelwin started in 2026 for B2B companies that paid an agency for a quarter of
  activity and ended where they started."
- **After:** "Kelwin started in 2026 for B2B companies that spend a quarter on activity and end it where they
  started."
- **Before** (audit FAQ, approved, row 1, under "How is this different from a lead generation agency?"): "An agency
  rents you activity, and the results stop the day the contract does. Kelwin installs the narrative system, channel
  orchestration and intelligence layer, and your team owns all three."
- **After** (for your yes): "We build and run the system behind what each buyer hears, whom you approach and why
  your deals close. If the plan is only more volume on the setup you have, we decline." The question can stay,
  because buyers ask it. The answer describes our standard, never theirs. The first draft ended "Outreach is one of
  its outputs.", which admits the overlap and doesn't say what the difference is.
- **Why.** "We're not an agency" is a line buyers have heard from agencies. Naming them keeps them as the reader's
  comparison. The reader's own scene (the quarter of activity, the flat chart, the founder writing every email at
  11pm) works without naming who sold it.

### T9 · Promises and proof shapes any firm could use

- **Before:** "Each report tells one client's case, with the problem, what we did and the numbers."
- **After:** "Each report follows one engagement from the readout to its latest Rebuild Sequence, published with the
  client's written yes."
- **Before:** "The founding team's background is in B2B sales and marketing."
- **After:** cut. It is true of every firm in the category (rule 14). About states the standards we keep instead
  (decision 2).
- **Before:** buying signs given as "such as a new sales hire or a funding round".
- **After:** "the changes that came before your own won deals". The method is the specific part; the two stock
  examples are what every outbound firm writes.
- **Why.** Interchangeable lines make Kelwin interchangeable. "Pipeline" as the promise also invites the question an
  agency is judged by: how many meetings, at what cost each.

### T10 · "Free" at the front of the line

- **Before:** "In the free System Audit, we compare how your team and your lost buyers describe you."
- **After:** "The System Audit compares how your team describes you with how your lost buyers do."
- **Why.** "Free audit" is the lead magnet of the lead-generation market. The homepage frames the audit as a fixed
  term, a gate and a request ("10 Working days · fixed scope"; "10 working days · one readout" with decision 4),
  which reads as an engagement a firm agrees to take on. The audit has no fee, and the audit page says so once.

### T11 · Giving away the recipe

- **Before:** "We split new accounts between the versions in the first emails we send for you and in your reps'
  first calls. We count which version brings more replies and second meetings from the companies you want."
- **After:** "The second edition keeps only what held up in first conversations with the accounts you want. It is the
  first edition your website, sales deck and call guides work from." How those conversations are split and counted
  goes in the proposal.
- **Before:** "If a domain's spam complaints near 0.3%, the limit Google set for bulk senders in 2024, we pause it."
- **After** (GTM Infrastructure, below the first screen): "Each week we check every sending domain against the
  limits the large inbox providers publish, and pause any domain that nears them."
- **Why.** Mechanics laid out step by step read as a method sheet a buyer can hand to an in-house hire. Your own
  instinct on the Lab ("to not give the entire idea away") applies to the method too. The specific part moves from
  how we do tasks to what we see and decide.

### T12 · The asset shrunk, and plain made small

- **Before:** "The work starts with four short documents. We keep them in one folder in your own drive, named
  Narrative OS."
- **After:** "Narrative OS has four parts: positioning, the message matrix, call guides and the proof list. Each
  edition records what changed and the buyer evidence behind it."
- **Before:** "The person pushing to buy from you" and "whoever signs the budget".
- **After:** "the champion" and "the economic buyer".
- **Before** (a section opener): "Here is a filled-in page for a pretend company."
- **After:** "The message matrix, filled in for an invented company."
- **Why.** "Short documents in a folder" is what a freelancer hands over. Explaining a sales leader's own words to
  them lowers both sides' standing. Plain for a senior buyer means exact.

### T13 · Who does what, as page content

- **Before:** "Your website · We rewrite the words on your homepage and main product pages. Your developer puts them
  live." and "We don't design logos, build websites or buy ads. We write the words, and your designer, developer or
  agency works from them."
- **After:** "Narrative OS is the source for your website, sales deck and call guides." The refusal becomes one of
  method or fit: "Each quarter we bring your leadership the lines the evidence no longer supports, favorites included."
  and "This work is for companies where several people sign off on each deal." (The first draft's "We don't write
  positioning from a leadership workshop alone" is what most positioning firms say.)
- **Why.** A row-by-row split of who does what is a statement of work. It places Kelwin in the client's line of
  suppliers (designer, developer, agency), each with a lane. The split is true and needed, in the proposal.

### T14 · Close service said as a service level

- **Before:** "If you write to us, a person answers. That isn't a feature. That's the point." (homepage letter, not
  among the seven lines; listed for a later yes)
- **After:** the reply time stays on Contact as a plain promise ("We reply within one working day if you're booking
  the audit or asking about an engagement, and within two otherwise."), never on the standards sheet. The close-service line is about who is in the room
  when the client decides something: "The founding team presents your readout, and is in the room at the kickoff, at
  day 90 and at each renewal." (decision 1)
- **Why.** "A person answers" and a reply time are what a buyer expects from any supplier. Close service, to a
  senior buyer, means senior people at the moments that matter, and an honest account of who does the work day to
  day (6.3, The team).

### T15 · The small firm's badges, and the extension-of-your-team line

Found in the first drafts of this round's documents, not in round 10.
- **Before:** "...and we run it with your team." (about 15 times across this round's four documents) · "If you request

## 4 · How high-touch sounds

### 4.1 Structure, never adjectives

The firms whose whole product is close service rarely call themselves premium, exclusive or white-glove. They
state facts that make the care believable. Bessemer Trust, for example, gives its ratio of clients to employees, and
Pictet says who owns and runs the firm (both as reported in search summaries; sources at the end). The reader
works out the care from the structure. Kelwin is new, so it has no ratios, tenure or retention figures to give. It
has standards it keeps, people it puts in the room and a record it writes. Those are what we say.

### 4.2 Five ways to make the client's experience felt

1. **Who is in the room, by role.** Senior people at the moments the client decides something is the strongest
   close-service line we have. "The founding team presents your readout, and is in the room at the kickoff, at day
   90 and at each renewal." (decision 1) It never claims that the founding team leads every review or runs every
   engagement: proposals name each seat by role, marked founding team or contractor (6.3, The team). On the client's
   side, use exact titles: your CEO, your head of sales, your RevOps lead. Never "an account manager", "a dedicated
   success manager", "your pod", "our experts", "founder-led" or "ourselves".
2. **Standards we keep, from decided facts.**
   - We present the readout live, on a 60-minute call, and don't send it ahead.
   - The audit needs two 90-minute sessions and read-only access to your CRM. We read the rest.
   - The system lives in your own accounts and documents.
   - We call only buyers you introduce us to (DECISIONS.md, decision 10).

   Reply times are a plain promise on Contact, never a standard (T14).
3. **The written record, which is also continuity.** "Each edition of Narrative OS records what changed and why."
   "The Rebuild Sequence keeps every quarter's decisions, so a new head of sales can see what was decided and why."
   "Each review ends with its decisions written down" is a new standard and needs your yes (decision 2). Continuity
   comes from the record. We never promise the same people for years.
4. **Write from the client's side of the table.** Say what the client sees, when, and what their team no longer
   has to carry. "Your leadership's time goes on decisions. We bring the evidence to each review." Test: a busy
   executive would notice it. Never claim a feeling ("you'll love", "stress-free", "peace of mind").
5. **Respect the reader's time on the page.** Short, exact, nothing said twice, one door per screen. That is itself
   the service.

### 4.3 What we never write

- **Concierge words:** white-glove, concierge, VIP, premier, dedicated, bespoke, tailored, seamless, "trusted
  partner", obsessed, "your success is our success", "we care as much as you do", above and beyond, hand-holding,
  peace of mind.
- **Servant phrases.** We speak as peers: no "happy to", "we'd love to", "don't hesitate", "feel free to reach out",
  "at your service" or "we're here for you".
- **The words for it.** "Premium" and "high-touch" describe what the reader should feel. They never appear on the
  page.

### 4.4 Off the page

The client feels close service mostly away from the site: in the proposal, the readout, the written record of
each review, and the small gifts you asked for. The same voice applies to all of them.

- **The proposal and the written record** use the client's own words for their business, name decisions and who
  made them, and give each next step a date.
- **The readout** opens with the finding that matters most, ranked, and says plainly if we're not the fix.
- **A gift** comes with two or three plain sentences about the client's work, signed "The founding team". Never
  "as a token of our appreciation", and never a word about Kelwin. Gifts are never described on the site. A gift
  described in marketing copy stops being a gift.

## 6 · The rules, revised, and the checklist

### 6.1 The rules

Thirty rules. Rules 1 to 24 keep their round-10 numbers so earlier documents still point at the right rule. Each is
marked **Kept**, **Revised** or **New**. Every rule has a test an editor can answer yes or no.

**1. Every claim names who does what to what.** *Revised.*
A claim has a doer (we, you, a role on your side, or one of our named assets) and an object. On first screens and
in section openers, the object is a client decision or a named asset: what buyers believe, which accounts matter,
why deals are lost, what changes next quarter. Tasks on channel things are described where the engagement is
described.
*Test:* underline the doer and the object in every claim. Rewrite if there is no doer, or if the doer is "it",
"something", "the layer", "the loop", "week one", or "the system" before its three parts are named on the page. In
the first screen and in each section's opening sentence, no object is a channel thing (email, inbox, domain,
sending, deck, slide, post, sequence, reply, meeting).
*Bad:* "We test the wording in your first emails, then rewrite your website, deck and call guides around the
winner." *Good:* "Each quarter we compare Narrative OS with the latest won and lost deals and change what no longer
holds."
*Changed because* the round-10 test asked every page to name who sends, calls and publishes, which turned pages into
a statement of work.

**2. Name the real thing.** *Revised.*
Use nouns a buyer could point at: the CRM stage, the deal notes, the readout, the 60-minute call, the board
meeting, the closed-lost reason. A named asset counts as a real noun when its first use on the page says what it
decides or shows. Abstract nouns used alone ("narrative", "architecture", "signal", "motion", "layer") still don't
count.
*Test:* each section has at least three real nouns, and one of them is new to that section. Each named asset is
defined the first time by what it decides or shows, never by its container ("folder", "file", "doc", "template").

**3. Numbers where we have them, and only real ones.** *Kept.*
*Test:* every number is a decided policy, a figure from a named source with its year (checked against the source
before the page goes live), or a figure inside a labeled specimen. Meeting lengths and per-package counts are
decided policy, but they go in the proposal (rule 16).

**4. Plain verbs, judgment first.** *Revised.*
Two kinds of plain verb. Judgment verbs: diagnose, find, trace, measure, compare, decide, rank, choose, refuse,
decline, present, review, and "run" for a system, a test or a review. Task verbs: write, send, set up, load, check,
fix, test, call, train. First screens and section openers lead with judgment verbs. Task verbs belong where the
engagement is described. Say who decides: "Your leadership decides at the quarterly review" or "We rank which
accounts come first". Never "together we agree".
*Test:* the first screen and each opener have at least one judgment verb, and no task verb whose object is a channel
thing. No word from the never list (6.2).

**5. Every section opens with a sentence a senior buyer can repeat.** *Revised.*
The test reader is a CEO or head of sales reading on a phone between calls. The opener says what we see, decide or
hold, in the reader's own professional words. The sentences in 6.2 are where writing starts.
*Test:* the opener has 20 words or fewer. It uses the reader's own terms (pipeline, champion, win rate, closed-won)
without explaining them. It has no word that shrinks our work ("short", "quick", "simple", "just", "little", "a
folder", "a few pages"). The readability score is a warning, not a gate: if running copy scores above grade 10,
look for long sentences; professional terms are not the problem.
*Changed because* "a ten-year-old could repeat" produced "Here is a filled-in page for a pretend company." Plain for
a child reads junior to a CFO.

**6. Explain our names and true technical terms once. Don't explain the reader's.** *Revised.*
Explain Kelwin's own names (2.4) and technical terms a CEO may not know (SPF, DKIM, DMARC). Don't explain pipeline,
champion, economic buyer, ICP, win rate, retainer or positioning.
*Test:* each of our names and each term on the "explain" list (6.2) has its explanation at first use. No term on the
"don't explain" list is explained.

**7. Give the reason where a CFO would ask why.** *Kept.*
A reason belongs where the order matters, or where we recommend or refuse something. Anywhere else, it is padding.
*Test:* mark every "because", "so" and "so that". Delete each in turn; if nobody would miss it, leave it deleted.
Wherever a CFO would ask "why?", a reason is there.
*Calibration:* "Nothing is built before the readout ranks what holds revenue back. A system built on the wrong
diagnosis has to be built twice." A CFO would ask why we diagnose first, so the reason stays. "...so nothing public is
rewritten twice", the first draft's reason for testing a message first, is cut: it reassures about saved work (rule
27), and nobody would miss it.

**8. The four buyer questions, answered in the right place.** *Revised.*
What do you need from me? What won't you do? What happens if it doesn't work? How is it bought? The Engagements page
and the proposal answer all four. The audit page answers what the audit needs from you. Each capability page answers
two: one refusal of method or fit, and what happens if this part isn't the problem.
*Test:* Engagements answers all four. Each capability page has one refusal of method or fit, and one answer for when
this part isn't the problem. No capability page carries engagement names as a menu, per-package rhythms, contract
length or who sends.

**9. Say the positive thing.** *Kept.*
*Test:* at most one knock-down pair ("X, not Y", "It isn't X. It's Y.") per page, and only when the reader really is
choosing between the two. Refusals and limits don't count.

**10. Lists only when the things are real.** *Kept.*
*Test:* for every list of three, ask whether there is a fourth item and whether one of the three is filler. Never
list abstract nouns. *Calibration:* "We keep the signal, language and learning from each quarter." fails. "Each
quarter we write down which accounts replied, which words buyers used and why deals were lost." passes.

**11. Show how it works. Don't reach for a metaphor.** *Kept, with additions.*
These count as metaphors: wired, loop, keys, floor, healthy, dies, smothers, armed, machine, engine, fuel, power (as
a verb), places (meaning where buyers see you), speak for you, and "run" for a document. The verbs carry, land,
survive and drift are on the never list. Fixed names such as Narrative OS are exempt.
*Test:* at most one hit per page, never in the sentence that says what we do, and the next sentence spells out the
mechanism. A hit inside an approved homepage line, a job title or a specimen quote doesn't count.

**12. Full sentences, except where the design expects short phrases.** *Kept.*
Running copy is full sentences: no fragment stacks, no colon reveals, no mic-drop closing lines, no long dashes.
Short phrases only in labels, headlines that name the offer, small sub-headings, spec rows, table cells, specimens,
captions, and button and link labels.
*Test:* no "—". Every colon in running text opens a real list of four or more items.

**13. Keep sentences short, and let the content set the rhythm.** *Kept.*
*Test:* the sentences under a headline and each opener have 20 words or fewer; body sentences 25 or fewer (field
notes excepted). At most one sentence of three words or fewer per page, and only as a direct answer. No three
sentences in a row start with the same word (a schedule is exempt). No more than three items in a row share one
shape, except in spec rows and tables.

**14. Make claims at their true size.** *Revised.*
No grandeur, no mystique, no softeners ("genuinely", "actually", "really", "truly", "simply"). No adjectives that

*Test:* search for the softeners and the standing adjectives; find none. Check each all-or-nothing word is literally

## 8 · Decisions for you

Four decisions only you can make. Each changes what the site may say. **Reconciled:** they now sit in DECISIONS.md,
the one list for this round: decision 1 here is part of decision 6 there, decisions 2 and 3 are decision 10, and
decision 4 is decision 1.

**1. Who is in the room.**
May the site say who from Kelwin is in the room?
*Proposed:* yes, in one sentence used everywhere it appears (About, Engagements, the message sheet, the letter on the
homepage): "The founding team presents your readout, and is in the room at the kickoff, at day 90 and at each
renewal."
*Why:* senior people at the moments a client decides something is the strongest close-service line we have, and a
buyer can check it. It must be policy, because buyers will hold us to it. It matches the lifecycle work: a member of
the founding team as the client's sponsor at those moments, and the engagement lead, today the GTM and
revenue-architecture specialist, a contractor, running the work week to week, named by role in every proposal. The
first draft said the founding team "leads every quarterly review", which the delivery model can't keep; a buyer who
has been sold by senior people and served by others knows that trap, and would feel it by day 90. "Each renewal"
replaces "the last meeting", which points at an ending. The first call isn't in the sentence: the homepage already
promises "a 30-minute call with the founding team", and offering a sales call as a service is a small firm's badge.

**2. The standards sheet, made public.**
May the site publish these as Kelwin's standards, numbered, on About and Engagements?
1. We don't name a client, or show a client's material, without the client's written yes.
2. We present every readout live, on a 60-minute call, and don't send it ahead.
3. The system lives in your own accounts and documents.
4. We don't buy or sell lists.
5. We publish no figure we can't source and no example we don't label.
6. If we're not the fix, the readout says so, and names what would be.
7. Each review ends with its decisions written down.

*Proposed:* yes to all seven. Items 2 to 6 follow decisions you have already made or lines you approved. Items 1 and
7 are new standards. With a yes to decision 3, the competitor rule joins them as item 8, and "We call only buyers you
introduce us to." (Narrative Systems' decision 7) as item 9. The competitor rule then covers every engagement, Launch
and the fixed-scope engagements included (OFFER 3.1, point 8).
*Why:* firms that sell close service show it as rules they keep, not adjectives. The first item also explains,
honestly, why the site shows no client names, without hinting at clients we can't show. The first draft had a reply
time as item 3; it moves to Contact (T14), because a reply time on a sheet of firm standards reads as a supplier's
service level.

**3. Competing clients.**
May the site say "We don't take on two companies that compete for the same buyers at the same time."?
*Proposed:* yes, on the standards sheet and Engagements.
*Why:* saying whom we take on is what separates a partner from a supplier. It is a policy a client can hold us to,
and it costs us a deal now and then, which is why a buyer believes it. The first draft also proposed "We take on a
limited number of engagements at a time." That is no longer proposed: with no number, it is the calm version of
"limited spots this quarter", nobody can check it, and a CFO reads it as a sales tactic. The capacity limit stays in
proposals, where a start date proves it.

**4. One name for one thing: the smaller engagements and the audit.**
The offer work proposes "fixed-scope engagements" for the smaller family and "retained engagements" for Launch,
Growth and Scaling. The homepage also calls the System Audit "A fixed-scope diagnostic" and "10 Working days · fixed
scope".
*Proposed:* take the offer work's names, and change the audit's two homepage tags to "A ten-day diagnostic" and "10
working days · one readout" (homepage lines 01d and 10c).
*Why:* a phrase that names one of our offers can't describe another, or a buyer reads the audit as a small paid
engagement. "Fixed scope" fits the paid family, where the scope and the fee are set in writing; what sets the audit
apart is its ten days and its one readout. The runner-up, "standalone engagements", avoids the clash, but it suggests
the work doesn't connect to the system.
