# The engagement lifecycle, version 2: one sale, one way of working

Round 11 · 19 October 2026 · revised after our stress test · for your decision

In round 10 you asked us to change the lifecycle. In your words: "we should run a proper, thorough sales process on
both types of engagements. We should not act too hard to differentiate our sales process and smack ourselves in the
foot just because of trying too hard." You want the customer experience "top-notch" and the customer "fully
satisfied", and you suggested "some minor, inexpensive gifting to the clients during the sales process, during the
engagement, or at the end of the engagement… almost less than $100".

This document replaces round 10's "two ways in, one spine". It covers both families of engagement: the three packages
(Launch, Growth and Scaling), which the offer proposes to call retained engagements, and the fixed-scope engagements
that replace Projects. Prices and terms come from the offer (OFFER.md). The two documents agree on every term, and the
decisions from both are listed once, in the offer's section 12. If a number changes, the steps here stay the same.

## 1 · The short answer

Use one sale and one way of working for both families: the same nine steps, the same standards, and the same people
at the same moments. Only the time changes.

- **A package starts with the free System Audit:** a 30-minute first call, ten working days, and the readout
  presented on a 60-minute call. **A fixed-scope engagement starts with a free scope check:** up to three working days
  on the one piece, and a 30-minute readout. If the scope check shows that more than one part is breaking, we say so
  and offer the audit instead, once. Under about $10,000, a shorter path: the written scope and the order form come
  with the scope readout.
- **Every call ends with a dated next step and a written recap within one working day.** Every readout ends with a
  one-page plan to decide. Every proposal is presented live, with one recommendation and one step down (under about
  $10,000, the call is offered, not required). No discount ever expires.
- **After signing:** a welcome note within one working day, and a kickoff that books every meeting of the term. A
  shared folder opens in the client's own accounts, and a one-page note goes out every Friday. Bad news comes from us
  first, by phone, within one working day. The last meeting is the best one.
- **A founding-team sponsor is in the room at the moments that matter:** the first call, the readout, the proposal,
  the kickoff, day 90, the renewal talk, the last meeting and any bad-news call. Day to day, the GTM and
  revenue-architecture specialist you have lined up leads the work, committed for the client's whole term, with a
  named backup. The proposal names that seat by role and marks it as a contractor.
- **Gifts are small, personal and about the work.** During the sale, only handwritten notes; readouts go as files,
  printed only on request. Physical gifts come only after signing, at most $100 per person a year. Public-sector,
  healthcare and finance clients get notes only, and no one ever gets cash or gift cards. Over a six-month package,
  gifts cost about $150–400 in all.
- **A fixed-scope engagement leads into a package only when the evidence says so,** at the check 30 days after
  handover. If the client signs a package within 90 days of handover, half the fixed-scope engagement's fee comes off,
  up to one month of the package's fee, for work the package reuses. The credit is spread over months 4, 5 and 6.

What we'd tell a buyer: "However big the engagement, it runs the same way. We look before we quote, you get a written
note every Friday, and if something goes wrong you hear it from us first, by phone."

## 2 · The sale: one process for both families

### 2.0 The nine steps at a glance

| Step | Packages | Fixed-scope engagements | The same for both |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 · First reply | An audit request | A message on Contact, with the engagement named | A personal reply. The site promises one working day for the audit and for a message that names an engagement, two for anything else. We aim for one working hour |
| 2 · First call | 30 minutes | 30 minutes | The same agenda, the starting price early, a dated next step, and a written recap within one working day |
| 3 · Diagnosis | The System Audit: free, ten working days, read-only | A scope check: free, up to three working days, read-only, on the one piece | We read before we recommend, and change nothing in their systems |
| 4 · Readout | 60 minutes, presented live | 30 minutes, presented live | The goals and measures agreed in their words, the written readout after the call, and a handwritten note |
| 5 · Plan to decide | One page | Four lines in the recap | Every step dated, with an owner on each side |
| 6 · Proposal | Within 5 working days of the readout | Within 2 working days; under about $10,000, sent with the scope readout, the call optional | Presented live: one recommendation and one step down, the team by role, and everything they could ever pay, on one line |
| 7 · Questions | The security pack, insurance, references | The same, shorter | Answered in writing within two working days |
| 8 · Signature | The first payment, then the kickoff | 20% on signing | No discount that expires |
| 9 · Welcome | Within one working day of signing | The same | A short note with the kickoff date and what we need in week 1 |

**Why one process.**
- In Rackham's study of about 35,000 sales calls, pushing for the close helped in small sales and hurt in large
  ones. Asking good questions and agreeing on the next step won large sales (Rackham, SPIN Selling, 1988, K).
- So the large-sale way of selling does no harm in a small sale, and we can use it for both.
- Customers reward consistency across the whole journey more than any single great moment (Pulido, Stone and
  Strevel, McKinsey, 2014, K; Rawson, Duncan and Jones, HBR, 2013, K).
- A client who has lived through our first call, readout, kickoff and Friday notes on a small engagement already
  knows the package.

**Why the time scales.**
- An audit and its proposal take about 13 contractor hours on a set template, about $1,700 at middle rates, plus the
  founding team's time on the calls (OFFER 2.2). Before a $6,000 engagement, that would be more than a quarter of the
  fee.
- A scope check takes about 3 contractor hours, about $375. Counted per signed client, if one in two signs: about
  $3,400 before a package, and about $750 before a fixed-scope engagement, about 12% of a $6,000 fee (our
  arithmetic).

**The sale itself builds the loyalty.** In CEB's study of more than 5,000 business buyers, 53% of loyalty came from
the sales experience, against 19% from the product or service (Dixon and Adamson, The Challenger Sale, 2011, K).
How we sell is part of what we sell.

### 2.2 The first reply

- **The buyer:** a reply from a person, not a template. It gives times for the call and says what the call covers.
- **We send:** the booking confirmation. It holds:
  - what to expect on the call;
  - one-page audit terms, signed with the booking: a mutual NDA; their data used only for the audit; our contractors
    bound by the same terms; nothing changed in their systems; our access removed, and confirmed in writing, at the
    readout; and EU transfer terms if EU contacts are in scope (the lawyer finishes them, OFFER 11);
  - a short note on what data the audit would read, where it is kept, and when our access ends (at the readout)
    (R10);
  - a link to the security pack (OFFER 7.7), and a line saying the audit can start from exported files while their
    security team reviews tool access;
  - one line on price: "On the call, we'll tell you where engagements start."

  For a fixed-scope engagement, the same note, sized to the one piece.
- **How fast:** one working day for the audit (confirmed in round 8) and for a message that names an engagement (the
  map's proposal, adopted here), and two for anything else. These promises sit on Contact. In practice we aim for one
  working hour on weekdays. In an audit of 2,241 US companies, firms that answered within an hour were nearly 7 times
  as likely to qualify the lead as firms that answered an hour later, and more than 60 times as likely as firms that
  waited a day or longer (Oldroyd, McElheran and Elkington, HBR, 2011, K).
- **In the room:** no call yet. A strong audit request gets its reply from the founding team; the rest from the named
  contractor who holds audit@ that day (DECISIONS.md, decision 6; BACKEND 4.5).
- **If it isn't a fit:** the "not a fit yet" screen and a check-in in about six months, as in 2.1.

### 2.3 The first call (30 minutes)

The same call for both families. The agenda:

1. **Minutes 0–3.** Who is on the call, by role. What the call will decide: which door fits, if any. It ends on a
   dated next step either way.
2. **Minutes 3–5. Where engagements start** (B8), in the same words every time: "Retained engagements start at $8,000
   a month on a six-month term, and the first three months cost less. Fixed-scope engagements start at about $5,000
   to $6,000, at a fixed fee. You get the exact fee on the proposal call." We say it early, so nobody gives us ten
   days and their CRM access without knowing.
3. **Minutes 5–15.** What they sell, to whom, and what changed that made them write now. What they have tried, and
   what happened.
4. **Minutes 15–20. How they decide.** Who signs, who else has a say, and whether legal, procurement or a security
   review is involved. MEDDICC, a common way of qualifying deals, calls this the "paper process"; it adds weeks to
   mid-market deals (K).
   - We also check for regulated fields: US healthcare companies that bill federal programs, broker-dealers,
     investment advisers and insurers.
   - Those clients get flat fees only (R10), and notes only as gifts (section 5).
   - Whether their security team reviews new suppliers, and whether their legal team allows LinkedIn tools on their
     people's profiles. If so, the security pack goes out that day, the audit can start from exports, and the
     hand-sent LinkedIn option is on the table (OFFER 3.4, 7.7).
5. **Minutes 20–25. Which door fits.**
   - If more than one part of how they sell is breaking (the words, the sending and the CRM, or the signals and the
     reporting), the System Audit.
   - If only one piece is breaking, a fixed-scope engagement, starting with a scope check.
   - If neither fits, we say so.
   - For a package, the size guide: Launch for a company where the founder still sells most deals; Growth once there
     are account executives and SDRs. Until Growth can be staffed, we say so: "Growth is the fit; the earliest start is
     [date]." The honest step down is Launch with add-ons, labeled as Launch, never as Growth (OFFER 3.1).
6. **Minutes 25–30. The next step, dated on both calendars.**
   - For the audit: the read-only access we need, and the readout booked ten working days after the access arrives.
   - For a scope check: the access we need, and the 30-minute readout booked.

- **The buyer:** 30 minutes with the founding team, a price early, and a straight answer on fit.
- **We send:** a recap within one working day. It gives what we heard, in five lines, and the next step with its
  date. Rackham calls a step the buyer commits time to an "advance". Good large-sale calls end in one (K).
- **How fast:** we aim to hold the call within five working days of the request (our proposal).
- **In the room:** Kelwin's sponsor, from the founding team (section 3.1).
- **If it isn't a fit:**
  - We say so on the call, and why.
  - If another kind of firm would serve them better (for example, one that only books meetings), we say what kind.
    We never name one on our pages.
  - They're offered the field notes.
  - If they miss the call: one offer to rebook, then a short note.

Buyer line: "On this call we'll tell you whether we're the right firm, and where our engagements start."

### 2.4 The diagnosis for a package: the System Audit (free, ten working days)

**What happens.**
- The audit takes ten working days from the day access arrives (approved).
- It needs read-only access to the CRM, the sending tools and domains, and the messages in use; or, until their
  security team approves access, exported files and screen-shared sessions. Their team gives us two working sessions
  of up to 90 minutes, as the audit page says today, and we gather the rest ourselves.
- Nothing is changed in their systems.
- We diagnose. The fix (the plan in detail, the documents, the running system) is the paid work. Blair Enns makes
  "diagnose before you prescribe" a rule for expert firms (The Win Without Pitching Manifesto, 2010, K).

- **The buyer:**
  - an access checklist on day 1;
  - the two working sessions;
  - a short Friday note on each of the two Fridays the audit spans: what we've read, what's left, what we need.

  That is the same Friday note every client gets (section 3.5), so the buyer sees how we work before they buy.
- **We send:** the checklist, the two Friday notes, and answers to their questions within one working day.
- **How fast:** the readout falls on the tenth working day after access arrives, and is booked on the first call. If
  access comes late, the date moves day for day, and we say so in writing.
- **In the room:**
  - Kelwin's sponsor, from the founding team, runs both working sessions, reviews every finding and presents the
    readout. Those hours are part of the sale.
  - The engagement lead (the GTM and revenue-architecture specialist) reads the deal notes, the messages and the CRM
    reports on a set template, about 6 hours, and the GTM engineer reads the systems, about 4. About 10 contractor
    hours in all, about $1,300 at middle rates (OFFER 2.2).
- **If it isn't a fit:**
  - If access hasn't arrived five working days after the call, we pause the audit and say so in writing.
  - If the audit shows that only one piece is breaking, we say so early, and offer the fixed-scope engagement that fixes
    it. The audit's findings then replace the scope check.
- **Capacity:** at most two audits a month, and one while two pilots run (OFFER 9.3).

**What it is worth.** This is for proposals only, never for the site, and never naming a competitor there. Other
firms charge:
- $3,200–5,000 to audit a CRM alone (Aspect, thisisaspect.com/pricing; RevOps HQ, revopshq.com/store/hubspot-audit;
  both S);
- $5,000 to onboard (RevPartners, revpartners.io/pricing, S; the page may show $12,900, and may date from about
  2022);
- $35,000 for a 6–8-week strategy and audit (Refine Labs, as reported by a competitor, saashero.net, S).

### 2.5 The diagnosis for a fixed-scope engagement: the scope check (free, up to three working days)

**What happens.** The same idea as the audit, sized to one piece:
- read-only access to the one thing:
  - email deliverability: the domains' records and a placement test;
  - a CRM setup plan: how the CRM is set up;
  - positioning and messaging: the website, the sales deck and a few recorded calls;
- about 3 hours of contractor time (OFFER 9.2);
- a short written finding, and a 30-minute readout.

For an event (conference activation or an executive dinner), the check is about dates and lists:
- the date leaves at least 8 weeks for a conference, or 10–12 for a dinner;
- whether their sending domains are already warm;
- who fits the room (our research on the fixed-scope market: domains take 2–4 weeks to warm; dinners take about 10
  weeks to fill).

**We say it honestly.** "This is a three-day look at one piece, not the full System Audit. If we see more than one
piece breaking, we'll tell you and offer the audit instead." We offer the audit once, and accept the answer.

**When we skip it, we say so.** We skip it when a System Audit has already covered the piece, or when the client is
already ours (a package client adding an engagement, or a client buying a second one). "We've already read this, so
there's no scope check. Here's the written scope."

- **The buyer:** a free look at the thing itself, before any price is final.
- **We send:** the access list, then a one-page written finding. Under about $10,000, the written scope and the
  order form come with it (2.11).
- **How fast:** the readout within three working days of access, and the written scope within two working days of the
  readout.
- **In the room:** the engagement lead, or the contractor who will do the piece, runs the check. Kelwin's sponsor
  presents the readout.
- **If it isn't a fit:** we say so, and the finding is theirs to keep.

**Why not the full audit.**
- A ten-day wait to fix one thing slows the buyer down.
- Indecision loses 40–60% of qualified deals, and one fix the research suggests is to limit how much a buyer has to
  explore (Dixon and McKenna, The JOLT Effect, 2022, K).

Buyer line: "Before we quote, we look at the thing itself, for free, and tell you what we found."

### 2.6 The readout

A package's readout takes 60 minutes, presented live, as you decided in round 9. A fixed-scope engagement's takes 30
minutes. The 60-minute agenda:

1. What you asked us, and what we read (5 minutes).
2. What we found, part by part: what is broken, where, and what it costs you (30 minutes).
3. What we would do first, and what you could do without us (10 minutes).
4. The goals and the measures of success, agreed in your words (10 minutes). Alan Weiss's rule: agree on goals,
   measures and their worth before writing a proposal, so the proposal only confirms what was agreed (Million Dollar
   Consulting Proposals, 2011, K).
5. The plan to decide, drafted together, and the next step dated (5 minutes).

- **The buyer:** what they couldn't see themselves, and goals written in their own words.
- **We send:**
  - the written readout within one working day after the call, whether or not they hire us (R10's proposal, settled
    in the reconciliation: it follows the live readout and is never sent ahead);
  - a printed copy only if they ask ("Would a printed copy help for your board?"). A readout holds their CRM's and
    pipeline's problems, and many offices are empty or have clean-desk rules;
  - the plan to decide;
  - a handwritten note within a week (section 5).
- **How fast:** as above.
- **In the room:** Kelwin's sponsor presents, and the engagement lead joins for the findings in their part, so the
  client meets the person who would run the work. The client's signer is invited.
- **If it isn't a fit:**
  - The readout says so, and points them somewhere better, as the site already promises. We make no pitch.
  - One personal check-in follows about 90 days later.
  - They join the field notes only if they agree (sitemap).

### 2.7 The plan to decide (a mutual action plan)

One page, drafted at the end of the readout and kept in a shared document. For a fixed-scope engagement, it is four
lines in the recap email. Each line has a date and an owner on each side:
- the proposal call;
- the questions still open, and who answers them;
- the security questions, the insurance certificates and the legal review of the master agreement;
- the buyer's procurement steps (supplier forms, tax form, bank details);
- who signs;
- the target start date and the kickoff.

**Why.**
- A buying group is usually 6–10 people, and 77% of buyers called their last purchase very complex or difficult
  (Gartner, The B2B Buying Journey, K).
- Buyers spend about 17% of their buying time with all the suppliers they consider, put together (same source).
  Most of the decision happens when we are not in the room, so the plan has to work without us.
- A dated plan removes the "what now?" that feeds indecision (JOLT, K).

- **The buyer:** a plan on their calendar, not ours.
- **We send:** the plan with the written readout, updated within one working day of any change.
- **In the room:** drafted on the readout call, by Kelwin's sponsor and the client's signer.
- **If it isn't a fit:** if a step slips past its date, we ask once what changed, and say how long the start date can
  hold.

Buyer line: "Here's the plan to a decision, on your calendar, not ours."

### 2.8 The proposal

The proposal is presented live on a call, never sent cold: 45–60 minutes for a package, 30 for a fixed-scope
engagement. The document follows the same day. The call takes place within 5 working days of the readout, or 2 for a
fixed-scope engagement. Under about $10,000, the written scope and the order form go out with the scope readout, and
the call is offered, not required (2.11). The starting price was given on the first call (B8), so the fee is no
surprise.

**What a package proposal contains:**

1. **Page one:** what we agreed at the readout (the goals, the measures, and what reaching them is worth), in their
   words.
2. **"What's fair about this"** (R10, updated for this round's terms):
   - no setup fee on a six-month term (the readiness fee applies only to month to month, or to leaving at day 90
     when we met every target);
   - tools, domains and data in their name from day one;
   - the pilot never costs more than three months at the full fee;
   - no meeting fee after month 3;
   - the right to leave at day 90 (on the open option, for any reason);
   - written targets for months 4–6, and the right to leave if the meetings target is missed two months running;
   - everything they could ever pay us, on one line;
   - their data used only to serve them, unless they say yes separately;
   - a handover month at no extra fee, if they ever want one.
3. **One recommendation and one step down.**
   - The recommendation is the package the readout points to, if we can staff it by the start date.
   - The step down is a smaller package, a package with fewer add-ons, or a fixed-scope engagement. It gets one
     line, at its price on the open option.
   - If the right package can't be staffed yet, we say so, with a real date (R10).
4. **The ways to start, in three columns, for the recommended package only** (OFFER 5.4): six months, fixed; six
   months, open at day 90 (in the middle); month to month. Each column gives four numbers: a pilot month, a full
   month, the amount paid at signing, and the most they can ever pay, revenue share included. We recommend the middle
   one on its merits: they can leave at day 90 for any reason. The caps, the readiness fee, the exit terms and the
   renewal ladder go on the order form.
5. **The team by role.** Each seat is marked founding team or contractor, with hours a month and its backup (R10).
6. **The pilot terms:** the pilot targets and both sides' duties, what counts as a held meeting, and the one sentence
   on the revenue share.
7. **The fee schedule:** one worked example, the line with everything they could ever pay (OFFER 5.4), and the tool
   stack: each tool, its estimated monthly cost, and its vendor's security page (R10, OFFER 7.7).
8. **Working with your team,** for a company with its own sales team (OFFER 3.6).
9. **The response standards** (section 6).
10. **The plan to decide,** updated.

On the call we also ask about funding stage or runway (R10). We never ask in writing.

**What a fixed-scope engagement's proposal contains:**
- the same page one, and a shorter "What's fair";
- the written scope: the fixed fee, the typical length, and what is in and out;
- the payment schedule: 20% on signing (it covers week one), 50% at the written go, 30% at handover (approved);
- the week-one go or stop, and the check 30 days after handover.

For an event, outside costs (the venue, the food) are billed at cost with the receipt. We write a target, never a
guarantee.

- **The buyer:** one clear recommendation, and a page they can forward to finance.
- **We send:** the proposal and the order form, the same day as the call.
- **In the room:** Kelwin's sponsor and the engagement lead. The buyer meets the person who will run the work before
  signing. A firm sold by senior people and staffed by others is a known warning sign for buyers (Maister, Managing
  the Professional Service Firm, 1993, K).
- **If it isn't a fit:** "no" is a fine answer, and the step down is on the page. If the timing is wrong, we say when
  we could start.

Buyer line: "We'll walk you through it, and tell you which one we'd choose in your place."

### 2.9 Questions, and references when we have none yet

**Questions.**
- Answered in writing within two working days.
- Ready before the first proposal: the security pack (OFFER 7.7). It holds a completed short security questionnaire
  in a standard format; the data processing agreement, listing each contractor's company and country; the access
  table (the least access each person needs, two-factor login, never a sole admin login held by us); the offboarding
  standard (access removed within a day of someone leaving, and confirmed in writing at the end); and the certificates
  for professional liability and cyber insurance.

**References.** We don't yet have client references we can share (please confirm: DECISIONS.md, quick answers), and we
never invent one (standing rule 7). We say so plainly: "We're early, so here is the person who will run your account,
what they did before us, and your right to leave if they change."

What we offer instead:
- the person who will run their account, met before signing, and, with that person's and the former client's consent,
  a reference for their own earlier work: labeled as their work before Kelwin, never as Kelwin's;
- that person's written commitment for the client's whole term, the backup for each seat named in the proposal, and
  the right to leave on 30 days' notice with no fee if the lead changes to anyone but the named backup;
- the written readout they already hold: our work, on their business;
- the pilot's written targets and the day-90 exit. For a fixed-scope engagement, the week-one go or stop, which costs
  week one only;
- everything they could ever pay us, on one line;
- the field notes, which show how we think;
- with your yes and the company's own written yes, a short call with a company whose audit you ran earlier
  (NEXT-STEPS, stage 8). The call is about what the audit was like, and is never described as a client result.

Never:
- a contractor's past clients presented as ours;
- "references available on request" when there are none.

**Once clients exist.**
- We ask each one in person whether they will take reference calls.
- At most one call per client per quarter, asked each time and thanked each time with a note.

### 2.10 The close

- **No pressure.**
  - No discount that expires, and no "this price is good until Friday".
  - Pushing for the close lowers success in large sales (Rackham, K).
  - It also makes the seller look self-interested, which lowers trust however good the rest is (the trust equation:
    Maister, Green and Galford, The Trusted Advisor, 2000, K).
- **Take the risk off the table instead:** the pilot, the exit, the caps, and the tools in their name (JOLT, K).
  Offer the ways to start as a fair menu, and recommend one.
- **Signature and first payment.**
  - The master agreement, the order form and the fee schedule are signed electronically. The site, proposals and
    emails say only "Kelwin". The contract's party line and signature block use the registered legal name, as the
    lawyer advises, because someone who signs for a company the other side doesn't know is an LLC can be held
    personally liable (R10; settled in the reconciliation, as the lawyer advises).
  - The first payment: month 1, plus a deposit of one pilot month: $8,800 at signing on Launch, fixed, or $10,400 on
    the open option. Later invoices are paid by automatic bank debit, authorized at signing, or by bank transfer if
    their finance team requires it (OFFER 7.3).
  - The kickoff waits for payment (R10).
  - A fixed-scope engagement: 20% on signing.
- **The buyer:** a welcome note within one working day, with the kickoff date and the list for week 1.
- **In the room:** Kelwin's sponsor, on the call where they say yes.
- **If they say no:**
  - We ask once what decided it, thank them in a handwritten note, and offer the field notes.
  - At most two follow-ups (our proposal), then one personal check-in about 90 days later (sitemap).
  - The written readout stays theirs.

### 2.11 How long the sale takes (our estimate)

**A package, typically:**
- the reply on day 1;
- the first call within a week;
- the audit, ten working days after access arrives;
- the readout;
- the proposal within 5 working days;
- questions, legal review and procurement, 1–3 weeks (the security pack and the tool vendors' reviews start during
  the plan to decide);
- the signature;
- the kickoff within 5 working days of payment.

That is about 5–8 weeks from request to kickoff.

**A fixed-scope engagement:** about 2–3 weeks.

**Under about $10,000, a shorter path.** The first call; the scope check; the written scope and the order form sent
with the scope readout; a proposal call only if they want one; the kickoff folded into the first working session. The
aim is a signature within five working days of the first call. The Friday note and the 30-day check stay, because
they are what make it the same process. Buyers expect the small door to be fast, and it is the way up into a
package.

**Day 0.** The welcome note, handwritten (section 5), and an email with the kickoff date and the list for week 1.

something concrete answer it.

**The kickoff** takes place within five working days of the first payment: 90 minutes for a package, 45 for a

2. Who is who, on both sides.
3. **Every meeting of the term booked on both calendars, there and then:** the working sessions, the monthly and

4. **The client room opened.** It is one shared folder in the client's own Google Drive, SharePoint or Notion, never

5. **The first 30 days, week by week.** This includes the 2–4 weeks while new sending domains warm up (R10). Said

6. **What we need, and by when** (R10):

7. **For a package** (R10):
   - the ICP written as fields that can be checked, with the levels that count;

   - the included show named and dated (OFFER 3.3);

8. **Working with your team,** for a company with its own SDRs, RevOps, product marketing or events team: the separate

9. **The baseline recorded:** meetings, pipeline and reply rates on day 1, so a later report has a real before and
   after (NEXT-STEPS, stage 8).
10. **The backup lead introduced,** and how to reach us, with the response standards (section 6).

Their policy on gifts and hospitality is asked once, through their supplier onboarding form (or ours if they have
none), never on the call, and recorded in our own files (section 5).

Chase and Dasu's other rules apply here (K):
- Get the unpleasant chores done early: all access in week 1.
- Let the client choose where the choice is real, such as which angle to test first. Choice builds commitment.

**In the room:** Kelwin's sponsor and the client's signer, the engagement lead, and the build team for the first
half.

### 3.3 The first 30 days

**Packages:**
- **Day 0:** the welcome note.

- **Day 20:** a working session on that draft.
- **Day 30:** the first monthly review.

- the positioning and messages (the Narrative OS) approved by week 4;

**Fixed-scope engagements:**

**Why so early.**
- The sooner a client sees something real, the more likely it stays. Customer-success practice calls this "time to

- The warm-up wait is the first low point, and people mind waits they didn't expect more than waits they did (our
  reading of Chase and Dasu, K; Heath and Heath, The Power of Moments, 2017, K).

Buyer line: "For the first few weeks you'll see us building, not sending. Here's what you'll see each Friday."

### 3.5 Reporting: the Friday note

The Friday note is one page, every Friday, for both families. It uses the same five headings every week, so the

1. What happened this week.
2. What we learned.
3. What happens next week.
4. What we need from you, with dates.
5. The numbers. For packages, from the live dashboard. For fixed-scope engagements, progress against the written scope.

- The engagement lead writes it, Kelwin's sponsor reads it before it goes, and it is filed in the client room.

  and continues in months 4–6 without fees (R10).

The site's current About page already shows a written note every Friday ("Every engagement, every week — what moved,
in writing"). This makes it true for both families.

### 3.6 The quarterly review, and the two questions

**The quarterly review.**
- 60 minutes, with Kelwin's sponsor and the client's signer, looking forward: what we learned, what we stop, next
  quarter's plan, and what we need from them.
- The week-11 pilot readout is the first one.
- Customer-success practice builds these reviews around the client's own goals, not around the supplier's activity
  (Mehta and others, 2016, K, weak).

**The two questions,** asked at day 90, at month 6, and at each fixed-scope engagement's 30-day check:
- "How likely are you to recommend us to a peer, from 0 to 10?" (the Net Promoter question: Reichheld, HBR, 2003, K)
- "What one thing should we change?"

Kelwin's sponsor reads every answer and replies to each. We change one thing each quarter because of them, and say
which.

**Why.** In Bain's survey, 80% of companies believed they gave a superior experience, and 8% of their customers agreed
(Allen, Reichheld, Hamilton and Markey, Bain, 2005, K). No score is published until it is real and the client agrees
(standing rule 7).

### 3.7 When something goes wrong

- **Tell them first, by voice.** A call within one working day of our knowing, before they find it themselves.
- **Something urgent: a call within two working hours.** Urgent means sending paused, a domain on a blocklist, a
  wrong message sent, a LinkedIn profile restricted, or anything else that touches their reputation.
- **Then in writing within 24 hours, in five lines:**
  1. what happened;
  2. why;
  3. what we have already done;
  4. what changes so it doesn't happen again;
  5. what it costs them, if anything, and how we make it right.
- **Kelwin's sponsor is on every bad-news call.**
- **Fix it and close the loop.** Service-recovery research advises acting fast and telling the customer what changed
  (Hart, Heskett and Sasser, HBR, 1990, K).
- **Don't count on a good recovery to win loyalty.** A meta-analysis found that a failure handled well lifts
  satisfaction, but not the wish to buy again (de Matos, Henrique and Rossi, 2007, K). Recovery limits the damage.
  It doesn't build loyalty.
- **No gift after bad news.** If money is owed, it is credited on the invoice.
- **Client delays are said the same week.** A date the client's delay moved shifts the dates that depend on it, day
  for day (R10). We say so in that week's Friday note, so it never surprises anyone at day 90.

Buyer line: "If something goes wrong, we call you before you find it."

**What happens** (as in round 10).

- It opens a window of five working days. On the open option, month 4's invoice goes out the day after the window

**What the client may do depends on how it chose to start** (OFFER 5.4 and 7.6). On Launch at $8,000:

- **Month to month:** there is no day-90 moment. Either side may end it on 30 days' notice, from the first day of
  month 2.

**Your fourth case** (staying at the readout; OFFER 5.4, decision 7):
- At the week-11 readout, a client on the open option that wants to stay may sign a 12-month term starting at month 4,
  on a one-page amendment. Months 4–6 then bill at the 12-month price, 10% off: $7,200 a month on Launch, saving

- If you'd rather reward a commitment to the rest of the six months only: half the gap, $1,200 on Launch, never all of
  it.
- We offer it once, at the readout, and never push it.
- A verbal yes can't remove a written exit (our research on terms; the lawyer should confirm).

**If the client leaves, it ends well.** People remember the end of an experience more than its average (the peak-end
rule: Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier, 1993, K).
- A written exit readout: what we built, what we learned, and what we'd do next, whoever does it.
- The sourcing list signed by both sides. The revenue share still applies to the companies on it (R10); after an exit
  for our miss, only to deals that close within six months of the meeting (OFFER 6.3).
- Everything is already in their accounts. Our access is removed, and confirmed in writing, on the last day.
- Month 3's meeting fees, and the readiness fee if it applies, are set against the deposit, and the rest is refunded
  (OFFER 8.3).
- The two questions, and a handwritten thank-you.

**For a fixed-scope engagement,** the check comes earlier: the week-one go or stop. Its "day 90" is the handover.

Buyer line: "At day 90 you get a written readout against the targets we set together. If you chose the open option,
you can leave then, for any reason."

### 3.9 Month 5: renewal (B5)

- **When:** renewal is proposed in month 5 (approved), on a call with Kelwin's sponsor and the client's signer. On
  Scaling, start in month 4, because larger accounts take longer to decide (our research on terms: software teams
  start about 90 days ahead on larger accounts, K). That moves your month 5 to month 4 on Scaling only.
- **Results first:** the call opens with the results against the targets written at day 90. The renewal is decided by
  months 1–5, not by the call: clients drift toward leaving unless they keep seeing value (Mehta and others, K, weak).
- **The options:** from the ladder written on the order form since day one (OFFER 7.5), or a step up or down, or
  upkeep:
  - month to month at the full fee plus 10%, after one grace month at the current fee;
  - six months at the full fee;
  - 12 months at 10% off;
  - 24 months at 15% off.

  On Growth, the 12- and 24-month prices wait until the first Growth client's hours are known. Leaving a longer term
  early pays back only the part of the discount not earned (OFFER 7.5).
- **Written reminders** go out on a schedule the lawyer sets, for example 45 and 30 days before month 6 ends, whatever
  has been said. The roll into month to month, and its price, are also printed next to the signature on the order
  form. As we understand New York's General Obligations Law, section 5-903, some automatic renewals of service
  contracts need a reminder 15–30 days before the date by which the client must give notice (K). The lawyer should
  say whether it reaches these contracts under Delaware law. The reminders avoid the argument either way.
- **If nothing new is signed** by the end of month 6, month 7 runs at the current fee, and then the engagement runs
  month to month at the month-to-month price, with 30 days' notice either side. No price changes without a month's

- **No gift while the renewal is open,** beyond a note.

Buyer line: "If we haven't signed a new term by month 6, month 7 runs at the same fee, then month to month, and we'll
remind you well ahead."

- **Make the last meeting the best one,** with Kelwin's sponsor and the client's signer (peak-end rule, K). It covers:
  - the results against the targets;
  - what the system does now that it didn't before;
  - the sourcing list, signed;
  - the plan for next quarter, whoever runs it;
  - the two questions;
  - the thank-you (section 5).
- **Retainer first.** What we built already lives in their accounts and documents, so we hand over only when they
  ask.
  - If they give notice and ask for a handover, the notice month is the handover month, at no extra fee: their
    operator is trained, and the six documents are brought up to date (R10).
  - A handover asked for in the middle of a term is priced as a fixed-scope engagement.
- **On the last day,** our access is removed and confirmed in writing. Their prospects' data is returned or deleted,
  as the data terms say (R10).
- **For fixed-scope engagements,** the handover meeting is the best meeting, and the check 30 days later follows
  (approved).
- **After the end:** a short note at 3 months and at 12 months, with a field note they'd find useful (our proposal).
  It costs nothing, and keeps the door open.

### 3.11 Asking for referrals and case studies

**Referrals.**
- **Ask only after a high point:** a day-90 readout that met its targets, the first deal won from a held meeting, the
  month-6 readout, or a fixed-scope engagement's 30-day check if the result held.
- **Ask once, in person, and be specific:** "Who's one other founder selling into your market who'd want this
  readout?" Then send a short note they can forward.
- **At most once a quarter** per client.
- **Never pay a person at a client for a referral.** Many states make it a crime to give an employee a benefit,
  without the employer's consent, to influence their work (for example New York Penal Law section 180.00, K), and most
  company codes forbid it. We thank them with a handwritten note. If an engagement follows, a gift within section 5's
  limits.
- **Why it's worth asking:** in one large study, referred customers were worth about 16% more and stayed longer. The
  study was of a consumer bank, not a B2B firm, so read it as a direction, not a number for us (Schmitt, Skiera and
  Van den Bulte, Journal of Marketing, 2011, K).

**Case studies (Reports).**
- **The publication clause goes in every contract** (NEXT-STEPS, stage 8; R10). Our proposed wording, for the lawyer
  to finish: "Kelwin may publish a factual report of this engagement, whatever the result. It is anonymized unless you
  agree in writing to be named. You approve the text before it is published. Nothing is given in exchange for it."
- **Never traded for a discount.** A fee cut in exchange for publishing is a connection that must be disclosed, and
  paying for testimonials that must be positive is banned. See the FTC's Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255) and its
  rule on reviews and testimonials (16 CFR Part 465, in force since 21 October 2024) (R10).
- **The baseline recorded at kickoff** makes a real before and after possible.
- **When to ask:** after the first measured result, not at signing and not on the last day. We draft it, they approve
  every line, and it runs under an institutional name, as Reports do.

### 3.12 Events: conference activation and executive dinners

Events follow the same steps, with these differences.

- **Dates drive everything.**
  - Conference work starts at least 8 weeks before the event, because new domains take 2–4 weeks to warm.
  - Dinners need 10–12 weeks, because filling the table takes about 10 (CXO & Co,
    cxoand.co/blog/how-to-plan-executive-dinner, S; our research on the fixed-scope market).
- **The client hosts.**
  - We plan, invite, brief and follow up, working remotely with the venue's own events team or a producer on
    contract.
  - We have no one on site, and we never suggest otherwise.
- **The venue's contract is always in the client's name.** If a venue will only contract with us, the client pays the
  deposit and the minimum first, and carries the venue's cancellation, attrition and liquor-liability terms (OFFER 7.2).
- **Dates are fixed in writing.** Each included show is named and dated on the order form at signing. A dinner's
  middle payment falls due once 8 guests have accepted, at least 21 days before (OFFER 4.2).
- **We write a target, never a guarantee.**
  - Meetings booked at a conference booth take place 40–60% of the time. Executive dinner guests attend 70–90% of
    the time (vendelux.com/event-marketing/b2b-event-marketing-benchmarks, S; Clutch Events, quoted by CXO & Co, S).
  - Text or WhatsApp reminders go only to guests who gave their number and agreed to reminders. WhatsApp requires
    opt-in (business.whatsapp.com/policy, S), and US texting law has no exemption for business numbers
    (wipfli.com/insights/articles/does-tcpa-apply-to-b2b-marketing-key-rules, S).
- **What counts as a meeting.** A meeting our outreach books at a show counts like any other held meeting. A seat at
  a dinner never does; a separate first meeting booked after it may (OFFER 6.3).
- **The 30-day check counts what follows.** It measures follow-up meetings and opportunities, not attendance: "the
  dinner doesn't generate pipeline — the follow-up does"
  (attendir.com/blog/executive-roundtable-vip-dinner-playbook, S).

## 4 · The upsell path: from a fixed-scope engagement into a package

### 4.1 When we propose it

- **At the check 30 days after handover,** and only if the evidence shows more than one part breaking (round 10's
  rule). We show the evidence, offer the System Audit once, and accept the answer.
- **Earlier only if the client asks.**
- **Never during the build, and never in a Friday note.**
- **If the scope check or week one already shows more than one part breaking,** we say so then, before they buy the
  one piece.
- **If the piece is holding and nothing else is breaking,** we say that too, and offer upkeep or nothing more.

**Why.** If every fixed-scope engagement ended with "you need a package", buyers would learn that the small door was a
trap (our research on premium practice).

**What usually leads where** (examples, not a script):

| Fixed-scope engagement | What the 30-day check shows | The next step |
|---|---|---|
| Email deliverability, or the outbound build | The domains are healthy, but replies show the message or the targeting is the problem | Launch |
| Positioning and messaging | The words are written, but nothing runs them | Launch, where the positioning becomes the Narrative OS unchanged (R10) |
| Conference activation | The meetings worked, and they want the same outreach all year | Launch |
| An executive dinner, or a series | The follow-up turned guests into opportunities | Growth or Scaling, where dinners become an add-on |
| The CRM setup plan, or the ICP and signal map | The system now counts correctly, but the pipeline is thin | Launch or Growth |

### 4.2 What the client has seen by then

By the 30-day check, the client has lived through every step a package uses:
- the first call, and its recap;
- a diagnosis, and a readout presented live;
- a plan to decide;
- a proposal presented live;
- a kickoff with every meeting booked;
- a Friday note every week;
- the response standards;
- the best meeting last (the handover);
- the two questions.

They have met Kelwin's sponsor and the engagement lead, and what we built lives in their accounts. So the package is
not a new supplier; it is more of what they already know. Consistency across the journey predicts satisfaction better
than any single moment (McKinsey, 2014, K; Rawson and others, HBR, 2013, K).

### 4.3 The path

1. The 30-day check.
2. The System Audit, still ten working days. It needs no first call, because the check served as one, and what the
   fixed-scope engagement already read isn't read again.
3. The 60-minute readout.
4. The plan to decide.
5. The proposal.
6. The package.

**What they already have shortens the start.** A finished positioning becomes the Narrative OS. Domains that are
already warm mean sending can start sooner than week 5. The pilot's targets are written from where they are.

**Upkeep** (from $3,500 a month, approved): if they are on it, it ends the day the package starts, with no notice
period.

### 4.4 The credit (recommended)

- **The amount:** half the fixed-scope engagement's fee, up to one month of the package's full fee, if the package is
  signed within 90 days of handover.
- **How it's paid:** in three equal parts, on the invoices for months 4, 5 and 6.
- **What counts:** only our fee, and only for work the package reuses: deliverability, positioning, the ICP and signal
  map, the CRM setup plan, win/loss, the operating model and the outbound build. Events (conference activation,
  dinners) earn no credit, and neither do upkeep fees or outside costs passed through at cost.

**Why months 4–6, not month 6 as in round 10.**
- In the offer's proposed terms, the deposit already pays more than half of month 6, which leaves too little to
  credit against. (In round 10, the deposit paid all of month 6, so the two already collided.)
- The credit lapses by itself if the client leaves at day 90, with nothing to claw back. Round 10's credit also lapsed
  at a day-90 exit.
- It softens the step from the pilot price to the full fee, which is the moment the client decides whether to stay.

**Why 90 days, not 60.** After the 30-day check, the audit, the readout, the proposal and the buyer's own legal review

**Why half.**
- The fixed-scope engagement was finished work, with value of its own. Crediting all of it would make it a free trial.
- The pattern is common. Aspect credits its audit fee against a rebuild hired within 30 days (thisisaspect.com/pricing,
  S). Software firms commonly credit a paid pilot against the first year (K).

Buyer line: "If you move to a package within 90 days of handover, half of this fee comes off months 4 to 6."

## 5 · Gifts

### 5.1 The rules

1. **Ask first, quietly.**
   - Ask once, through the client's supplier onboarding form (or our own supplier form if they have none), about their
     company's policy on gifts and hospitality. Never on the kickoff call: asking there makes people wonder why we are
     planning gifts. Record the answer in our own files, not in the client room.
   - Before that, nothing goes beyond a note, so no policy can be broken during the sale.
2. **Caps.**
   - During the sale: handwritten notes only. This departs from your note, which suggested gifts during the sale too: a
     gift while a decision is open is exactly what buyers' gift rules and bribery laws target (below).
   - After signing: at most $100 per person per year, and never above the client's own cap. The running total per
     person is checked against the gift log before anything is sent; anyone who received the signature gift gets at
     most $70 at the end.
   - Most gifts cost $5–75.
3. **Notes only, unless their policy clearly allows more,** for:
   - public bodies and anyone working in government;
   - employees of state-owned companies;
   - US healthcare clients, and anyone they sell to;
   - finance clients (broker-dealers, investment advisers, banks and insurers).

   Why these groups:
   - Abroad, employees of state-owned companies count as foreign officials under the US Foreign Corrupt Practices
     Act, which has no minimum amount (FCPA Resource Guide, 2020, K).
   - US federal employees may accept at most $20 a gift and $50 a year from one source (5 CFR section 2635.204, K).
   - FINRA Rule 3220 caps gifts to people at broker-dealers at $100 a person a year (a rise to $300 was proposed; we
     haven't checked its status, K).
   - The pharmaceutical and medical-device industry codes (PhRMA, AdvaMed) bar gifts to healthcare professionals
     that aren't educational (K).
4. **Never:**
   - cash, gift cards or vouchers, at any value;
   - alcohol, by default;
   - anything with our logo on it;
   - gifts to family members;
   - anything sent to a home address the person didn't give us for that;
   - any gift while a proposal, a renewal or a bad-news period is open, beyond a note;
   - gifts to the client's prospects for taking a meeting. Round 10's definition of a held meeting already says "No
     gifts or payments were given for attending".
5. **Disclose.**
   - Every gift is given openly, sent to the office and from the firm. It carries a handwritten note signed by the
     person who did the work.
   - It goes in the client's own gift register if they keep one, and in our gift log: date, person, company, item,
     cost and reason.
   - The FCPA guide treats gifts given openly and recorded in the giver's books as a sign of proper giving (K).
6. **The team over one person, where possible.** Something the whole team shares reads as thanks for the work, not as
   a favor to one person.
7. **Local and chosen.**
   - A shop in the client's own city, and something they mentioned.
   - Not in December by default: a note on the anniversary of the start means more than one more box in the busiest
     week (Ruhlin, Giftology, 2016, K, weak).
8. **Nothing about gifts on the site, and never a mention in a proposal.** A gift described in advance stops being a
   gift (the voice work agrees).

**Why these rules.**
- Buyers' gift rules exist because a gift can create a sense of obligation (Cialdini, Influence, 1984, K). That is
  why we give nothing while a decision is open: a gift then could look like it is buying one. Our gifts thank people
  for work already done. None of this reasoning appears in anything a client sees.
- Bribery between private companies is a crime in many US states when the employer hasn't consented (New York Penal
  Law section 180.00, K). It is also a crime in the UK (Bribery Act 2010, sections 1, 2 and 7, K).
- The UK's official guidance says that reasonable, proportionate hospitality is a normal part of doing business
  (Ministry of Justice, 2011, K).
- A US lawyer should confirm this policy before it reaches a client.

**Tax.**
- A business may deduct at most $25 per person per year for gifts (Internal Revenue Code section 274(b); IRS
  Publication 463, K). So a $100 gift costs about $100.
- Budget the full amount.
